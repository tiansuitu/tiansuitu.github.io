  • Three ways to place \(\limsup\): random variables, events and probabilities

    November 4, 2026 (expanded November 27, 2026)

    Motivation: Durrett, Probability: Theory and Examples (5th ed.), Exercise 3.4.2(a), p. 127. Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be i.i.d. with \(\mathbb E X_1=0\) and \(0<\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)<\infty\). Put \(S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\) and \(Y_n=S_n/\sqrt n\). The exercise asks us to use the central limit theorem and Kolmogorov's zero-one law to show that

    \[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}Y_n=+\infty \qquad\text{a.s.} \]

    The difficulty I want to remember is the distinction between taking a limsup of random variables, taking a limsup of events, and taking a limsup of their probabilities. These are not interchangeable operations.

    1. Event-level comparison. For any sequence of real-valued random variables \(Y_n\) and any fixed \(x\in\mathbb R\), consider the three events:

    \[ \begin{aligned} B_x&=\{\omega:\limsup_n Y_n(\omega)>x\},\\ A_x&=\limsup_n\{Y_n>x\},\\ C_x&=\{\omega:\limsup_n Y_n(\omega)\geq x\}. \end{aligned} \]

    Here \(A_x\) means that the threshold \(x\) is exceeded infinitely often along one fixed outcome. In \(B_x\) and \(C_x\), we first compute the numerical limsup along that outcome, then compare it with \(x\). The relationship is

    \[ \boxed{B_x\subseteq A_x\subseteq C_x.} \]

    Both inclusions can be strict, even for deterministic random variables. If \(Y_n=x+1/n\), the threshold is exceeded at every index but the numerical limit equals \(x\). Thus \(A_x=\Omega\) and \(B_x=\varnothing\). If \(Y_n=x-1/n\), the threshold is never exceeded but the numerical limit is again \(x\). Thus \(C_x=\Omega\) and \(A_x=\varnothing\).

    The same sandwich holds if the middle event is \(\limsup_n\{Y_n\geq x\}\). The subtlety in all cases is the boundary where the numerical limsup equals \(x\).

    2. Probability-level comparison. Returning to the notation used for Exercise 3.4.2(a), define \(D_n=\{Y_n\geq x\}\). The three quantities I want to distinguish are

    \[ \begin{aligned} p_1(x)&=\mathbb P(\limsup_n Y_n\geq x),\\ p_2(x)&=\mathbb P(\limsup_n D_n),\\ p_3(x)&=\limsup_n\mathbb P(D_n). \end{aligned} \]

    The first is the probability of a statement about a numerical limsup. The second is the probability of infinitely many threshold crossings. The third is the limsup of a deterministic sequence of marginal probabilities. In particular, the third expression is not itself an event.

    \[ \boxed{p_1(x)\geq p_2(x)\geq p_3(x).} \]

    The first inequality follows from the event-level inclusion \(\limsup_n D_n\subseteq\{\limsup_nY_n\geq x\}\) and monotonicity of probability. The second is exactly Durrett Exercise 2.3.1, p. 58:

    \[ \mathbb P(\limsup_n D_n) \geq\limsup_n\mathbb P(D_n). \]

    No independence is required for either comparison. They need not be equalities: \(Y_n=x-1/n\) gives \(p_1=1\) and \(p_2=0\). For independent Bernoulli random variables \(Y_n\) with parameter \(1/2\) and \(x=1\), the second Borel–Cantelli lemma gives \(p_2=1\), whereas \(p_3=1/2\).

    Why useful in Exercise 3.4.2(a)? The CLT gives \(Y_n\Rightarrow N(0,\sigma^2)\). Since the limiting normal distribution has no atoms, for each finite \(x\),

    \[ p_3(x)=\lim_n\mathbb P(Y_n\geq x) =1-\Phi(x/\sigma)>0. \]

    Here \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function. The intermediate infinitely-often event bridges the CLT's information about marginal distributions and the desired information about sample paths. The comparison yields \(p_1(x)>0\). The remaining task is to justify the tail measurability needed for Kolmogorov's zero-one law and obtain the almost-sure conclusion for all thresholds. Importantly, although the increments \(X_n\) are independent, the partial sums \(S_n\) and the events \(D_n\) are not generally independent; one cannot simply invoke the second Borel–Cantelli lemma on them.

    Connection to almost-sure convergence. My original November 4 note used the errors \(W_n=\lvert Y_n-Y\rvert\) and a threshold \(\varepsilon>0\). If \(Z=\limsup_n W_n\), then

    \[ \{Z>\varepsilon\} \subseteq\{W_n>\varepsilon\text{ i.o.}\} \subseteq\{Z\geq\varepsilon\}. \]

    The middle event is the object relevant to Borel–Cantelli. The extended real-valued random variable \(Z\) describes the limiting upper error; \(Y_n\to Y\) a.s. if and only if \(\mathbb P(Z=0)=1\). See also What Does an Event Occurring Infinitely Often Actually Mean? for the underlying set-theoretic intuition.

    To revisit: Prove the two event inclusions from their quantifier definitions and the probability inequality in Exercise 2.3.1. Then finish Exercise 3.4.2(a), checking the hypotheses of the zero-one law carefully. Keep the distinction between the two sandwiches explicit.
